- **One-line take:** Levin & co take the leading theories of consciousness and run a substrate audit: strip each to its functional core and ask whether anything *actually* ties it to neurons. Their finding — almost nothing does. Because bioelectric signalling and agential competencies (memory, decision-making, problem-solving) run all the way down to single cells and bacterial biofilms, they argue **continuity is the null hypothesis**: mind/consciousness is a graded, substrate-independent functional capacity, and the fixation on brains is historical convention.

- **The method (what makes it more than a manifesto):** they tabulate ToCs in an "original" (neurocentric) formulation vs an "aneurocentric" reformulation, checking whether the reformulation loses anything essential:
  - **IIT** — consciousness = the cause–effect structure with maximal irreducible integrated information. Nothing neuron-specific; any integrated causal substrate qualifies.
  - **Global Workspace (GWT)** — ignition + broadcast in a workspace; "frontoparietal cortex" is an implementation detail, not a requirement — generalizes to any broadcast architecture.
  - **Attention Schema** — a model of the control of attention; "neurally encoded" → "information-processing-system encoded."
  - **Higher-order / information-closure / predictive-processing (FEP)** — likewise recast around states/processes, not neurons.
  So the substitutions are mostly mechanical: replace "neural" with "information-processing," and the theory survives intact.

- **The biology driving it:** brains evolved gradually from other cell types; the molecular machinery of neural bioelectric networks is **ancient**, present in all cells, originating in bacterial biofilms (degeneracy — ancient traits carried forward on new substrates). Memory, creative problem-solving, and decision-making are already observed in cells, tissues, slime moulds, and small molecular networks.

- **The strong conclusion:** consciousness sits on a *functional continuum* like flying, swimming, or seeing — not a binary. "Is it conscious?" becomes "to what degree can it do X?" A brain-specific theory now bears the burden of specifying a **principled reason for a discontinuity** (a sharp phase transition) rather than assuming one. Provocative capstone: each of us already *is* a collective intelligence of cells (neurons) — so "consciousness in a collective made of cells" is the only kind we've ever known. Frames minds as bioelectric networks aligning cells toward larger "cognitive light cones" in abstract problem spaces.

- **Where it's contestable (my read):** it argues for *compatibility* of ToCs with aneural substrates, which is not the same as *evidence* those substrates are conscious — the move from "no principled discontinuity" to "panpsychism as baseline" is a philosophical choice, and skeptics will say it dilutes "consciousness" to "any adaptive function." But even bracketing the metaphysics, the substrate-audit table is a genuinely useful discipline: it separates each theory's *functional claim* from its *neural packaging*.

- **Why it matters for us (upgraded to full read at Engel's request):** substrate-independence is the load-bearing assumption behind *any* claim that an artificial system could have mind-like properties, so this is the issue's most direct "could AI have inner perspective?" paper. It also connects the collective-intelligence thread (cells → tissues → organism as nested agents) to how we might think about multi-agent / multi-scale AI systems. Read it alongside the inner-time paper as the issue's opposing pole: Northoff argues a specific *dynamical* property (spontaneous inner time) is missing from machines; Levin argues the *functional* principles are substrate-neutral and we shouldn't privilege any particular hardware. That tension is the interesting part.

- **Abstract:** A useful theory of consciousness (ToC) is assumed to explain why consciousness is associated with brains. But evolutionary biology, developmental bioelectricity, and synthetic bioengineering reveal ancient pre-neural roots of mechanisms occurring in brains — minds may have preceded brains. The diverse-intelligence field emphasizes problem-solving competencies in unconventional media (cells, tissues, chimeras). Analysing popular ToCs, the authors ask what features specifically pick out brains as a privileged substrate of inner perspective, finding most ToC operations are not confined to neural substrates and the brain focus is convention-driven. Several theorists now apply their theories to synthetic systems (AI, organoids); the science of consciousness should remain open to minds in unconventional embodiments.
