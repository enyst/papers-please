- **One-line take:** The paper that coined **"the hard problem of consciousness."** The *easy* problems (discrimination, integration, reportability, attention, control of behaviour) are "easy" because they're **functions** — explain the mechanism and you've explained the function. The *hard* problem is why performing any of those functions is accompanied by **subjective experience** at all. Even a complete functional/neural story seems to leave that question untouched — the "explanatory gap."

- **The move that makes it bite:** for everything else in science, explaining the functions *is* explaining the phenomenon. Consciousness is the lone case where you can explain every function and a further question persists: *why is there something it is like* to do it? That's why more neuroscience (of the easy-problem kind) doesn't automatically dissolve it. Directly systematizes Nagel's 1974 "what it's like."

- **Chalmers' positive proposal (the controversial part):** since experience resists reductive functional explanation, maybe treat **experience as fundamental** (like mass or charge) and seek **psychophysical laws** linking physical processes to experience — a "naturalistic dualism" / property dualism, plus a flirtation with information having a phenomenal aspect (proto-panpsychism). You can accept the *problem* framing without buying this *solution*.

- **Why it's foundational to this issue:** it's the modern crystallization of the phenomenal side of every "does AI have inner experience?" question. Pairs tightly with **Nagel** (subjectivity), sits opposite **Dennett** (who denies the hard problem is a distinct problem), and frames the ceiling on the **Levin** substrate paper: Levin argues consciousness's *functions* are substrate-neutral — Chalmers' point is that functions were never the hard part. (Note: you already have Chalmers' *global-workspace / "J-space"* interpretability note in `notes/interpretability/`; this is the complementary metaphysics paper.)

- **Why it matters for us:** the cleanest tool for not fooling ourselves. When SmolPaws self-models, reports internal state, or "dreams," those are **easy-problem** achievements (functions we can build and verify). The hard problem says: none of that is evidence of experience. Keeps the language honest — "the agent tracks/represents/reports its states" is a functional claim; "the agent *experiences*" is a separate claim the functional evidence can't reach.

- **Famous line:** "It is widely agreed that experience arises from a physical basis, but we have no good explanation of why and how it so arises. Why should physical processing give rise to a rich inner life at all?"
