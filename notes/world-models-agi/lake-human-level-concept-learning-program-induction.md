- **One-line take:** The empirical demonstration behind the Lake et al. manifesto: model a concept as a **probabilistic generative program** and you get **one-shot learning** — from a *single* example of a novel handwritten character, the model classifies, generates new examples, and even invents new concepts, at human level, fooling judges in a "visual Turing test." Concept formation as *program synthesis*, shown to work.

- **The method — Bayesian Program Learning (BPL):** a character is generated by a structured stochastic program:
  - **compositionality** — characters are built from reusable **parts → subparts → strokes** and spatial relations; new concepts recombine existing primitives.
  - **causality** — the model captures *how the character is actually drawn* (the pen's causal process), not just how the finished image looks. Modeling the generative cause is what makes it generalize from one example.
  - **learning-to-learn** — priors over strokes/relations are learned from background characters, so a new alphabet needs almost no data.
  - Inference runs Bayesian: given one image, infer the latent program most likely to have produced it.

- **The results (why it landed in Science):** on **Omniglot** (1623 characters, 50 alphabets — the "transpose of MNIST": many classes, few examples each), BPL does one-shot classification near human accuracy and **far above** deep-learning baselines of the time; in generation tasks, human judges couldn't reliably distinguish BPL's novel characters from human-drawn ones. Concept learning, generation, and creative recombination from one shot.

- **The point that matters (vs deep nets):** contemporaneous deep learning needed thousands of examples per class and couldn't generate/recombine like this. The lesson isn't "symbolic beats neural" — it's that **structure (compositional + causal generative programs) buys extreme sample efficiency and flexible reuse**. Structure is the thing, and it's learnable/Bayesian, not hand-coded per concept.

- **Why it's foundational to this issue:** it's the concrete, quantitative instance of "world model = structured causal theory" that the RSTA **theory-based RL** and **representation-complexity** papers argue for at the level of tasks/RL. Where Othello-GPT shows structure can *emerge* from pure prediction, BPL shows structure *designed in* gives one-shot human-level concept learning. Two routes to the same moral: representation structure is what generalization runs on.

- **Why it matters for us (concept formation):** the cleanest existence proof that concepts-as-programs enable the fast, one-shot, recombinable learning Engel cares about — the opposite of statistical averaging over many examples. Design implication for agents: represent a learned concept/skill as a *small generative/causal program* you can run, recombine, and infer from one instance — compact, transferable memory, not a pile of episodes. Direct kin to the skills work in `notes/skills/`.
