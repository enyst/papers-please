- **One-line take:** Rogers' Paradox: cheap social learning yields no fitness benefit over individual learning (because social learners can copy stale info in a changing world). This paper re-runs it for the age of **humans learning from AI that is learning from humans** — and models the negative feedback loops that closed loop can create in a society's collective world model.

- **Why it's genuinely interesting (and a near-miss for full-read):** it's the most *societal-systems* paper in the issue, and topical — model collapse / epistemic feedback when AI both learns from and teaches humans. It asks what "levers" stakeholders have to keep the human–AI collective world model healthy. Relevant to the "what happens as agents mediate more human knowledge" question.

- **Status:** abstract-only — I ranked it just below the full-read set (it's simulation-based social science rather than agent-mechanism), but it's the strongest of the "collective" cluster for our interests. **Flag:** good full-read candidate if the human–AI epistemic-feedback theme is one you want to pursue.

- **Abstract:** People learn by conducting experiments and by copying others; these choices shape a population's collective understanding. Alan Rogers' simulations showed cheap social learning yielded no fitness benefit over individual learning — Rogers' Paradox — spawning decades of work on when social learning is favoured. The authors revisit it for human–AI interaction, extending simulations to a simplified network of humans and AIs learning together about an uncertain world. They examine how learning strategies affect the equilibrium of a society's 'collective world model', assess levers available to stakeholders, model negative feedback loops from humans learning socially from AI, and pose open questions for the framework.
