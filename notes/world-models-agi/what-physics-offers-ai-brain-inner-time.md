- **One-line take:** A Dreyfus-flavoured argument that current AI is "locked out of time and world": machines lack the brain's spontaneous, scale-free intrinsic dynamics ("inner time") that let it actively *participate* in the world's physical time (entrainment — e.g. neural/mental activity following music's rhythm). Without being-in-time, they can't acquire tacit/implicit knowledge or behave flexibly in a continuously changing world.

- **Why it's worth logging:** it's the issue's strongest "embodiment/temporality is the missing ingredient" position, grounded in real neuroscience (scale-free activity, variability, entrainment) rather than pure philosophy. A useful articulation of the *temporal* dimension the intro paper flags as often-missing from AI world models.

- **Abstract (condensed):** Computing can learn from physics in how it conceives time — as dynamics, the changing patterns of activity over time. The brain's intrinsic spontaneous activity and 'inner time' are used for dynamic input processing so it can 'participate' in the world's physical time (e.g. neural entrainment when listening/dancing to music shapes conscious experience). Scale-free activity and variability reflect an intrinsic temporal structure central to encoding input dynamics, actively modulated via a 'hidden dynamic repertoire'. Current computing devices (classical or non–von Neumann) lack spontaneous activity and inner time, so they can neither actively encode input dynamics nor participate in physical time — effectively 'locked out of time and world', preventing acquisition of tacit knowledge and flexible behaviour in a changing world.
