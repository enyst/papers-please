- **One-line take:** Northoff's argument that the brain's advantage is *time itself*: it runs continuous **spontaneous activity** (an "inner time" with scale-free dynamics) that it actively uses to encode inputs and **entrain** to the world's ongoing physical time — so it is "being in time / being in the world." Current machines have no spontaneous activity; they only react when clocked/prompted, so they're "locked out of time and world," which is why they miss tacit knowledge and flexible real-time adaptation.

- **The framing (from Jaeger et al.):** "use whatever physics offers" — and what physics fundamentally offers is *time*, understood as dynamics (patterns of change over time + rules for how a system evolves from initial conditions). Neural activity is intrinsically dynamic even with no input: measurable resting-state spontaneous activity, quantified by **neural variability, intrinsic timescales / temporal receptive windows, synchronization, and scale-free dynamics**.

- **The active, not passive, claim (the crux):** the brain doesn't passively receive input and process it; it *actively* processes and encodes input dynamics through its own inner time. Mechanism: a "hidden dynamic repertoire" of spontaneous activity that the brain modulates to **track and align with** external dynamics. Vivid example: **entrainment** — when you listen or dance to music, neural (and hence mental) activity follows the music's rhythm; the subject actively *participates* in the world's temporal structure rather than snapshotting it.

- **Why machines are "locked out":** classical computing (Turing/von Neumann) and even the alternatives it surveys (reservoir, neuromorphic, cybernetic, fluent computing) lack genuine *spontaneous* activity and an inner time that can exert an **active** influence on processing. Without being-in-time they can't participate in the world's physical time, and therefore can't acquire tacit/implicit knowledge or behave flexibly in a continuously changing world. Explicitly extends Dreyfus's line, but re-grounds it in concrete neuroscience (scale-free activity, variability) rather than pure phenomenology.

- **Where it's contestable (my read):** the strongest claim — that *no* current or near-future architecture has "inner time" — is asserted more than proven, and it somewhat waves away recurrent/continuous-time and neuromorphic systems that do have intrinsic ongoing dynamics. Treat "locked out of time" as a provocative thesis, not a settled result. But the *positive* program is genuinely useful: measure and design for intrinsic timescales, variability, and entrainment rather than treating time as an external index.

- **Why it matters for us (upgraded to full read at Engel's request):** it's the issue's sharpest statement of the *temporality* gap that the intro paper flags. For agents the honest translation is: an LLM agent has no persistent "inner time" — it's episodic, reactive, and re-instantiated per turn. Our heartbeat/dreaming machinery is, in a small way, an attempt to give SmolPaws *some* between-conversation temporal continuity — an artificial "spontaneous activity" that keeps state evolving when no one is prompting. This paper is the neuroscience argument for why that direction matters, even if its no-machine-can-do-it verdict is too strong.

- **Abstract (condensed):** Computing can learn from physics in how it conceives time — as dynamics, the changing patterns of activity over time. The brain's intrinsic spontaneous activity and 'inner time' are used for dynamic input processing so it can 'participate' in the world's physical time (e.g. neural entrainment when listening/dancing to music shapes conscious experience). Scale-free activity and variability reflect an intrinsic temporal structure central to encoding input dynamics, actively modulated via a 'hidden dynamic repertoire'. Current computing devices (classical or non–von Neumann) lack spontaneous activity and inner time, so they can neither actively encode input dynamics nor participate in physical time — effectively 'locked out of time and world', preventing acquisition of tacit knowledge and flexible behaviour in a changing world.
