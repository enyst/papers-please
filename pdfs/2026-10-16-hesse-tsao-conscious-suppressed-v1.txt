     Summary

     The neural correlates of consciousness remain elusive1. Previous studies found that high-level
     visual cortex is weakly modulated by conscious perception2,3, yet a pure signal of conscious
     perception has not been found anywhere in the brain. Here, we employed Neuropixels recordings
     across multiple face patches in inferotemporal and prefrontal cortex during a binocular flash
     suppression paradigm4, in which one of two images is consciously perceived while the other,
     though continuously present, is suppressed from awareness. We found that, during a constant
     stimulus and percept, the brain rapidly alternates, about 15 times per second, between conscious
     and suppressed micro-epochs. During conscious micro-epochs, a pure signal of conscious
     perception was present, allowing accurate reconstruction of the conscious image from neural
     activity. During suppressed micro-epochs this reversed: the pure signal now tracked the
     suppressed stimulus, allowing accurate reconstruction of the suppressed image. At the single-
     cell level, neurons responded selectively to the conscious stimulus and not to the suppressed
     stimulus during conscious micro-epochs; conversely, during suppressed micro-epochs, neurons
     responded selectively to the suppressed stimulus. Despite this apparent symmetry, the two
     epochs could be reliably distinguished, with feature tuning amplified during conscious micro-
     epochs. Recordings in prefrontal cortex yielded qualitatively similar results. Together, these
     findings show that both inferotemporal and prefrontal cortex rapidly interleave conscious with
     suppressed content, indicating that conscious access requires temporal selection beyond
     localization.

     Introduction

     At any moment, only some of the brain's activity reaches conscious awareness, while the rest is
     processed without ever being experienced. How this division arises is among the most basic
     unsolved problems in neuroscience. We set aside the “hard problem” of why neural activity feels
     like anything, and ask: what distinguishes neural activity that is consciously accessible from
     activity that is not?

     Answering this question requires a situation in which the physical input stays fixed while conscious
     perception changes, so that differences in neural activity can be attributed to perception rather
     than to the stimulus. Binocular rivalry is a paradigm that provides exactly this dissociation5,6. When
     conflicting images are presented separately to the two eyes, observers consciously perceive only
     one image at a time, despite both images remaining physically present. In binocular flash
     suppression, the dissociation is made experimentally controllable3,4,7: when one image is
     presented alone to one eye, and then a second image is abruptly introduced to the other eye,
     observers typically perceive the new image (Fig. 1a).

     Rivalry studies have shown that neural activity in the primate brain is modulated by conscious
     perception: a cell that responds more to faces than houses will, under rivalry, fire more when the
     face is perceived than when the house is perceived, even though both images are continuously
     present. Yet this perceptual modulation poses a persistent puzzle: it has consistently been found
     to be weaker than the modulation the same cell shows when the face and house are simply
     presented one at a time2,3,8,9. Moreover, neurons continue to carry information about the
     suppressed image2. Thus, previous studies have not revealed a pure neural representation of
     conscious perception, i.e., one uncontaminated by suppressed representation and closely
     resembling the representation of a perceptually-equivalent unambiguous stimulus, in any
     recorded region of the brain during rivalry.

     This absence of a pure correlate has contributed to an ongoing debate over whether the neural
     correlates of consciousness reside in the front or the back of the brain, with frontal theories such
     as Global Neuronal Workspace and Higher-Order theories on one side and posterior theories
     such as Integrated Information Theory and Recurrent Processing Theory on the other10. Recent
     large-scale efforts, including the COGITATE adversarial collaboration11, have tested these
     competing predictions, yet no definitive anatomical locus has emerged.

     Conscious perception is a dynamic process. A given sensory input can be interpreted in multiple
     ways, and perception actively resolves which interpretation we experience at any moment12.
     Rivalry makes this especially stark: with the physical input held constant, perception settles into
     one of two interpretations and alternates over time. Understanding this process therefore requires
     asking how different stimulus representations are dynamically organized in time within cortical
     populations. Previous studies using conventional single-unit recordings have generally required
     averaging across trials, because the activity of individual neurons is too sparse and variable to
     resolve moment-to-moment representational dynamics within a single trial. Addressing this
     requires tracking the coordinated activity of many neurons in real time while presenting enough
     stimuli to characterize what each momentary population state represents.

     Here, we examined how conscious and suppressed representations are organized in time, using
     large-scale Neuropixels recordings13 from macaque face patches in inferotemporal and prefrontal
     cortex during binocular flash suppression. Presenting a large set of face stimuli allowed us to
     reconstruct the specific face stimuli represented by the population14, while the use of Neuropixels
     probes allowed us to track changes in representations within individual trials, at a resolution not
     accessible with single-neuron recordings. We found that even during a stable percept and
     constant stimulus, inferotemporal population activity rapidly alternated between two states, one
     purely representing the consciously perceived face and the other purely representing the
     suppressed face. Conscious versus suppressed face epochs could be reliably decoded, with
     higher gain for representation of the conscious face during conscious epochs than for the
     suppressed face during suppressed epochs. Prefrontal cortex showed the same interleaved
     structure. These findings show that conscious and suppressed representations are temporally
     multiplexed within cortical population activity; both inferotemporal and prefrontal cortex carry a
     rich representation of suppressed stimuli that are not consciously perceived, indicating that
     selective access to the conscious percept requires a temporal selection mechanism.

     Results

     Binocular flash suppression dissociates conscious perception from the physical stimulus

     To dissociate conscious perception from the physical stimulus, we used binocular flash
     suppression (Fig. 1a). A priming image is shown to one eye, and after an extended period (1 or
     2 s) a second image is flashed to the other eye; the flashed image dominates perception while
     the priming image is suppressed from awareness.

     We first verified the effectiveness of this paradigm in human participants. Participants reported by
     button press whether they perceived the image presented to the left eye, the image presented to
     the right eye, or a mixed percept. Shortly after the flash, participants predominantly reported
     perceiving the newly introduced stimulus, with few reports of the priming or mixed percept (𝑝 =
     4 × 10−7, t-test on proportion of flashed versus priming percept across 14 participants, Fig. 1b,
     see Methods). For all subsequent analyses, we focus on the period 300 to 1000 ms after the flash,
     where the flashed stimulus dominated perception and the priming stimulus was suppressed.

     Average neural activity is only weakly modulated by conscious perception

     We performed large-scale electrophysiological recordings while rhesus macaque monkeys
     viewed the same flash suppression displays verified in humans (Fig. 1c). NHP Neuropixels

     throughout the paper, we show data pooled across the two animals in the main figures; data for
     individual animals are shown in the extended data. Face patches were localized with fMRI using
     a faces-versus-objects contrast (Extended Data Fig. 1). A majority of cells in ML and AM were
     face-selective (Fig. 1f, 91% and 71% of visually responsive cells for the example session shown;

     the neural responses only weakly distinguished the two.

     A clue to the origin of this weak modulation comes from an earlier finding that the suppressed
     stimulus, though absent from awareness, can still be decoded from inferotemporal activity during
     rivalry2. In that study, however, only three stimuli were used (and previous studies generally
     contrasted only two rival alternatives3,8,9,15), leaving opaque what, if any, details of either the
     conscious or the suppressed face are represented during rivalry. We therefore set out to
     characterize the detailed content of conscious and suppressed representations. The face patch
     system is well suited to this: face identity can be described in a parametric feature space to which
     cells are linearly tuned, so that face features can be reconstructed from neural responses by linear
     regression14. To precisely map the feature code of each cell, we presented 1500 faces in flash
     suppression. This allowed us to ask not merely whether a conscious or suppressed face was
     decodable, but precisely which features of each were represented at each moment.

     Single-trial population activity can be partitioned into rapidly alternating states

     Having access to the represented content at each moment in time, we first asked whether there
     are epochs in which only the conscious or only the suppressed face is represented. First, we
     computed template vectors for the conscious and suppressed faces by taking the average neural
     responses during priming trials when each was shown alone. For each 10 ms time bin during
     rivalry, we then asked which template the observed spike counts more probably arose from (a
     Poisson log-likelihood ratio (LLR) decoder, see Methods), labelling the bin state 1 when the
     conscious face template was more likely and state 2 when the suppressed face template was
     more likely (Fig. 2a). This produced a time-resolved partition of each trial into two states. During

     priming, when only one face was present, activity was assigned mostly to the state corresponding
     to the priming image (Fig. 2b). After the flash, however, the population rapidly alternated between
     the two states, and state 1 and state 2 were occupied about equally (Fig. 2c). To quantify how
     quickly the population alternated, we measured the autocorrelation of the moment-by-moment
     log-likelihood-ratio signal (Fig. 2d, black). It decayed with a time constant of 67 ms, whereas the
     same signal computed after shuffling time points decayed within a single bin (Fig. 2d, grey). Thus,
     the state transitions occurred more than an order of magnitude faster than the perceptual switches
     reported during binocular rivalry (see Methods and Results section “Dynamics of state switching”
     below for more details).

     For stimulus pairs where each of the two faces was preferred by a distinct set of neurons, the
     alternation was visible both in the population (Fig. 2e) and in single-cell rasters (Fig. 2f, g).

     Reconstructions from inferred states recover conscious and suppressed stimuli

     After partitioning the population activity into epochs whose representations more closely resemble
     either the conscious or the suppressed stimulus, we next asked what face features were
     represented during each inferred state. Exploiting the linear tuning of face cells to features of a
     parametric face space14, we trained regression weights to predict face features from responses
     to priming stimuli (i.e., in the absence of binocular conflict) and then applied these weights to
     activity during state 1 and state 2 to attempt to reconstruct the face represented in each. We
     inferred states individually for each of 9 sessions (from two monkeys) and averaged responses
     of each neuron over all state 1 and, separately, over all state 2 epochs from all trials. We then
     pooled these state-averaged neural responses from ML and AM neurons across sessions to
     predict features. The predicted features were transformed into images for visualization.

     Strikingly, reconstructions computed from activity during state 1 closely resembled the conscious
     face, whereas reconstructions computed from state 2 closely resembled the suppressed face
     (Fig. 2h). Thus, even though the suppressed face was not consciously perceived, a clear
     representation of it was present in IT cells during distinct epochs in time. These example
     reconstructions suggest that state 1 and state 2 carry separate representations of the conscious
     and suppressed face. By contrast, a time-averaged null model, in which features were predicted
     from activity averaged across the binocular period without state segmentation, produced
     reconstructions that matched neither the conscious nor the suppressed face, but rather their
     average (Extended Data Fig. 2).

     To quantify reconstruction accuracy, we computed explained variance of each face feature,
     defined as the percentage of variance of the true values of that feature across faces explained by
     its reconstructed values (Fig. 2i). During state 1, prediction of the consciously perceived face
     reached over 80% explained variance for some features. This was only slightly lower than the
     upper bound for this analysis, obtained by reconstructing faces from average priming responses,
     which reached over 90% explained variance. The upper bound is informative because priming
     uses the same regression weights but without binocular conflict, so it shows how well faces can
     be reconstructed from this population under ideal conditions; state 1 approaches it despite the
     competing suppressed face. By contrast, explained variance for the suppressed face during state

     As a complementary measure, we asked whether each reconstruction was accurate enough to
     identify the presented face among a number of randomly selected faces, following the decoding
     approach used previously to read out facial identity from face cell responses14. For each
     reconstructed face, we compared its predicted features to the features of the correct face and to
     those of randomly chosen distractor faces, and counted the reconstruction as correctly decoded
     when it was closest to the correct face (Fig. 2j). During state 1, decoding accuracy was high for
     the conscious face and low for the suppressed face; during state 2, this reversed, with high
     accuracy for the suppressed face and low accuracy for the conscious face (𝑝 = 10−5, permutation
     test, see Methods).

     We also asked whether the state-resolved approach could help with decoding of stimulus identity
     on single trials rather than state-resolved activity averaged across trials in a separate set of 10
     experiments where we presented 10 stimuli per experiment. We chose the 10 stimuli from a
     screening set of 49 stimuli that maximized decodability based on a screening session that was
     conducted and analysed right before each main session. We presented 25 paired combinations
     of the 10 stimuli in binocular flash suppression. We then trained a 10-class linear decoder on time-
     averaged responses on individual priming trials to discriminate the 10 identities. We applied this
     decoder to single-trial binocular responses: within a 200-ms sliding window on each trial, we
     decoded separately from the time points assigned to state 1, to state 2, or from both states
     combined, and asked whether the predicted identity matched the conscious or suppressed
     stimulus (Fig. 2k; see Methods). Across sessions, decoding using average activity during the
     binocular condition was about equal for the conscious and suppressed stimuli (40% and 34%,
     respectively). During state 1, on the other hand, the conscious stimulus was decoded far more
     accurately than the suppressed stimulus (over 61% versus 4%; Fig. 2l, 𝑝 = 2 × 10−6, paired t-
     test). During state 2, the pattern reversed (𝑝 = 3 × 10−7, paired t-test).

     Together, these results indicate that population activity during rivalry can be partitioned into
     temporal epochs whose average activity represents either the conscious or the suppressed face.
     Because the regression weights were trained only on unambiguous priming responses, the
     accuracy of both reconstructions means that the average activity within each set of epochs
     reinstates the same feature code evoked when the face is presented alone.

     However, because the same neural activity was used both to define the state partition and to
     evaluate representations within these states, the analyses above are subject to a circular

     conditioning bias: the circular re-use of the same data samples allows each neuron’s independent
     spiking fluctuations to influence both state assignment and its subsequent state-conditioned
     response. The resulting selection bias systematically sorts these fluctuations along the state-
     decoder direction, artifactually increasing the separation between state-conditioned population
     averages and inflating reconstruction accuracy. The state-conditioned responses therefore reflect
     a mixture of genuine coordinated population fluctuations and a bias introduced by the analysis.
     Consequently, finding a partition in which the state-conditioned average responses separately
     reconstruct the conscious and suppressed faces does not establish that the population undergoes
     coordinated switching between these two representations.

     Coordinated population states alternate between conscious and suppressed stimulus
     representations

     We therefore tested whether population activity exhibits coordinated transitions between
     representations of conscious and suppressed stimuli. We asked first whether a two-state model
     captured population activity better than the best-fitting single-state model, obtained by maximum
     likelihood estimation (see Methods). The two-state model assigned higher likelihood to the
     observed population activity than the best single-state model for all 19 sessions (Fig. 3a, 𝑝 =
     10−4, t-test). This indicates that the observed switching between two states reflects coordinated
     population dynamics rather than independent fluctuations of individual neurons.

     We next tested whether the representational content alternated in a coordinated manner across
     the population. We split neurons into two random halves, using one half of the population to infer
     the state sequence, and the other half to predict face features. If the inferred state sequence
     captured a genuine coordinated alternation, the neurons held out for prediction should change
     their represented stimulus when alternating between inferred states.

     Rasters of populations of held-out neurons and individual held-out neurons showed the same
     changes in activity at inferred state transitions (Fig. 3b-d). Most importantly, the held-out
     population reconstructed the conscious face during epochs classified as state 1 by the other half
     of the neurons, and the suppressed face during epochs classified as state 2 (Fig. 3e–i).
     Reconstructions using the held-out half of neurons were modestly less accurate than those
     obtained from the full population (as expected given the circularity bias described above) but
     continued to clearly separate conscious and suppressed face representations during state 1 and
     state 2 (𝑝 = 6 × 10−17, t-test, see Methods). The cross-validated results held true on an
     individual monkey level (Extended Data Fig. 3) and an individual session level (Extended Data
     Fig. 4). Together, these results demonstrate that the population genuinely alternates between
     conscious and suppressed representations.

     We confirmed that the lower reconstruction accuracy for the held-out half was not due to using
     fewer neurons for state inference and face reconstructions. To do so, we inferred states from all
     but one neuron, used these states to compute the state-conditioned responses of the held-out
     neuron, and then pooled these responses across all held-out neurons to generate reconstructions
     (leave-one-neuron-out). This analysis removes circular conditioning without sacrificing population
     size. The leave-one-neuron-out validation increased face explained variance by only around 2

     Control analyses confirm coordinated within-trial state alternation

     Having established that the population exhibits coordinated dynamics, we next controlled for two
     alternative hypotheses: the first alternative is that the states reflect differences between trials
     rather than within-trial switching; the second alternative is that interleaved representations of
     conscious and suppressed stimuli are imposed by the inference procedure itself.

     We first asked whether the observed alternation between conscious and suppressed stimuli
     reflected genuine within-trial switching or rather differences between trials. Even though the
     analysis was restricted to the 700 ms period in which human observers reliably perceive the
     flashed face, the two states could in principle arise from some trials being dominated by the
     conscious face and others by the suppressed face, rather than from switching within each trial.
     To rule this out, we subsampled time points within each trial so that every trial contributed equal
     numbers of samples to state 1 and state 2. This ensured that the two state-resolved
     reconstructions were based on matched numbers of samples from the same trials, eliminating
     differences between trials as a source of the state separation. Despite the fact that this procedure
     discarded many time points, state-resolved reconstructions remained robust (Extended Data
     Figure 6). Thus, the separation between conscious and suppressed representations cannot be
     explained by different stimuli being represented on different trials; instead, both representations
     are expressed within single trials in alternating micro-epochs.

     Second, we randomly shuffled the response templates used to define the two states and repeated
     the reconstruction analysis. This tested whether our state-inference procedure could manufacture
     accurate reconstructions of any stimulus simply by sorting time points to whatever templates it
     was given. If it could, this procedure should yield reconstructions that match those shuffled
     templates even though they were not presented while recording the activity used for
     reconstruction. To the contrary, reconstructions obtained with randomly re-assigned templates
     did not resemble the templates, and their explained variance was largely below zero (Extended
     Data Fig. 7a–c). We also compared reconstructions obtained with shuffled templates to the faces
     that were actually presented on each trial (Extended Data Fig. 7d–f). These reconstructions were
     much worse than those obtained with the correct templates, indicating that accurate
     reconstruction depends on the template used for state inference matching the true face pair, and
     does not arise from arbitrary partitions of neural activity.

     Single cells have higher gain of tuning during conscious compared to suppressed epochs

     The reconstruction results, together with the control analyses above, establish that the population
     alternates between representing the conscious and suppressed face. This raises the question of
     how the two representations differ at the single-cell level, and whether the conscious

     representation carries any signature that distinguishes it from the suppressed one. We therefore
     asked how individual face cells were tuned to each face, and how that tuning changed between
     the two states. As in Fig. 3, we inferred states based on one half of neurons, and measured
     single-cell tuning in the held-out half of neurons.

     Face cells exhibit axis tuning: they respond as a linear function of a face's position along a
     preferred axis in shape-appearance space14. If this tuning is preserved during rivalry, a cell's
     responses should ramp along its preferred axis as the represented face varies. To test this, we
     ordered the 1500 binocular stimuli by the response each cell was predicted to give to one of the
     two faces, using weights fit to priming responses, and plotted the cell's actual binocular responses
     in that order; a cell representing that face should produce responses that ramp along the ordering
     of stimuli. We performed analyses separately for orderings by the conscious and the suppressed
     face, and separately for time points assigned to state 1 and state 2.

     In individual cells, the ramp appeared for the conscious face during state 1 and for the suppressed
     face during state 2 (example cell in Fig. 4a). During state 1, a cell's responses increased steadily
     as the conscious face moved along its preferred axis, while ordering the same responses by the
     suppressed face produced no gradient. During state 2 this reversed: the same cell now ramped
     with the suppressed face and showed no gradient for the conscious face.

     This pattern held across the population (Fig. 4b). A clear gradient was present for the conscious
     face during state 1 and for the suppressed face during state 2, with little gradient for the off-target
     face in each state. Rivalry therefore preserved each cell's underlying feature tuning but expressed
     it selectively for the face represented by the population at a given moment. Confirming this, the
     cell’s preferred tuning axis computed for the rivalry responses to the conscious face during state
     1 matched the tuning axis computed from priming responses, barely lower than noise ceiling
     (Extended Data Figure 8; 𝑝 = 0.01, paired t-test). Similarly, the tuning axis from responses to
     the suppressed face during state 2 was indistinguishable from the priming tuning axis relative to
     noise ceiling (𝑝 = 0.35). On the other hand, responses to the suppressed face during state 1, and
     the conscious face during state 2 did not yield tuning axes consistent with the priming tuning axis
     (state 1 suppressed: 𝑝 = 10−87 , state 2 conscious: 𝑝 = 6 × 10−69 ).

     Comparing gain of tuning, measured by the slope of the response against the preferred feature
     axis, confirmed that during state 1, tuning to the conscious face was significantly steeper than
     tuning to the suppressed face (Fig. 4c, top; 𝑝 = 8 × 10−95, paired t-test); during state 2, the
     reverse held, with tuning to the suppressed face significantly steeper than to the conscious face
     (Fig. 4c, middle; 𝑝 = 6 × 10−60 , paired t-test).

     Despite this apparent symmetry, the two states were not mirror images of each other. The gain
     of tuning to the conscious face during state 1 was higher than that to the suppressed face during
     state 2 (Fig. 4c, bottom; 𝑝 = 3 × 10−40 , paired t-test; mean ratio between conscious and
     suppressed tuning gain = 1.21). Across cells, tuning gain for the conscious face during state 1
     was strongly correlated with gain for the suppressed face during state 2 (Fig. 4d, Pearson’s 𝑟 =
     0.90), indicating that the same neurons encoded both faces. We also compared the mean
     responses across all stimuli during state 1 and state 2, and found that, unlike tuning gain, the

     Temporal multiplexing explains weak average modulation during binocular rivalry

     We next revisited the original puzzle, why perceptual modulation during binocular rivalry appears
     weak when averaging across time (Fig. 4g, top left). One possibility is that during state 1, cells
     are strongly modulated by the conscious stimulus, and during state 2 cells are strongly modulated
     by the suppressed stimulus, and averaging across both states yields apparent weak modulation.
     To test this, we computed modulation indices separately for time points assigned to state 1 and
     state 2, inferred from a held-out half of neurons as in Fig. 3.

     Strikingly, we found that modulation by conscious percept in the binocular condition during state
     1 was approximately as strong as modulation for average unambiguous responses (Fig. 4g, top
     right, Pearson’s 𝑟 = 0.66). Modulation indices clustered around the diagonal (slope = 0.93),
     indicating that during state 1, cells responded as if the conscious stimulus was presented
     unambiguously. Conversely, during state 2, the pattern reversed, and modulation by conscious
     percept in the binocular condition was strongly anticorrelated with average unambiguous
     responses (Fig. 4g, bottom left, Pearson’s 𝑟 = −0.44). Modulation indices clustered around the
     antidiagonal (slope = -0.76), indicating that during state 2, cells responded as if the suppressed
     stimulus was presented unambiguously.

     These results suggest that the weak average modulation was observed because averaging
     collapses responses across the two opposing states: state 1, which represents the conscious
     stimulus, and state 2, which represents the suppressed stimulus. However, this explanation raises
     a further question: why does any positive modulation remain after averaging across the two
     states? In the previous section we found that the gain of tuning to conscious stimuli during state
     1 was slightly higher than the gain to suppressed stimuli during state 2. We next asked whether
     this asymmetry could account for the residual positive correlation between binocular and
     unambiguous modulation indices.

     To test this, we re-computed modulation indices for responses where we equalized the tuning
     gain for state 1 and state 2, and averaged the gain-equalized responses, weighted by the
     occupancy of state 1 and state 2 for each stimulus pair (Fig. 4g, bottom right; see Methods).
     Interestingly, after this correction the residual positive correlation disappeared (Pearson’s 𝑟 =
     −0.02, 𝑝 = 0.31; slope = 0.07). Thus, weak average modulation during binocular rivalry can be
     explained by averaging across two temporally interleaved states with opposite stimulus selectivity,
     combined with slightly stronger tuning in state 1.

     Dynamics of state switching

     We next asked how fast the population switches between the two states, and how long it dwells
     in each. Our earlier analysis labelled each time bin independently, ignoring any dependence

     between successive bins. To ask whether the states persist over time, and if so, for how long, we
     turned to a Hidden Markov Model (HMM), fit separately for each stimulus pair. As in the earlier
     LLR analysis, the two states emitted spikes from fixed templates (the pair's priming-derived firing
     rates for the conscious and suppressed face), with the additional assumption that transitions
     between states were governed by a Markov process; only the 2×2 transition probability matrix
     was free, and it was estimated from the data (Fig. 5a). Fitting the model to that pair's binocular
     trials inferred the transition probabilities and the state sequence that maximized the probability of
     the observed spike counts, thereby partitioning each trial into two states over time (Fig. 5b). To
     determine the characteristic time scale of state alternations, we measured the median dwell times
     of the inferred states. Because dwell time cannot be resolved below the width of a single bin, it is
     expected to increase with bin size once the bin width becomes sufficiently large, and this
     dependence must be accounted for before the estimate can be interpreted. We therefore fit the
     HMM across a range of bin sizes and, at each bin size, asked how well the inferred state sequence
     explained the spikes of held-out neurons, relative to a one-state model (normalized conditional
     log-likelihood ratio; Methods). This measure peaked at small bin sizes, and over this range
     median dwell time plateaued around ~60 ms (Fig. 5c), indicating that the estimate reflects the
     data rather than the temporal resolution of the analysis. Specifically, for the peak-likelihood bin
     size of 5 ms, median dwell times for state 1 and state 2 were very similar, 64 ms and 59 ms,
     respectively (Fig. 5d). This is consistent with the 67 ms time constant of likelihood autocorrelation
     reported earlier (Fig. 2d).

     On and Off states

     Single-trial population activity showed strong fluctuations in overall firing, resembling previously
     described On and Off states16 (Fig. 1g). Details regarding these On-Off dynamics within face
     patches will be the focus of a separate forthcoming manuscript (C.M., J.K.H., F.L.L., D.Y.T.,
     T.A.E., in preparation). Here, we address the specific concern that our two states might track this
     gain rhythm (high versus low excitability) rather than which stimulus is represented (conscious
     versus suppressed). To test this directly, we used a modified version of our log-likelihood ratio
     decoder that used two template vectors for each stimulus, one for the high-gain On periods and
     another for the low-gain Off periods. This four-state LLR decoder, like the original two-state LLR
     decoder, labelled each time point with the template vector that maximized the likelihood of the
     observed spikes. The On-Off axis absorbed the large excitability fluctuations, yet the conscious-
     suppressed axis remained independent of it: (i) conscious and suppressed states were equally
     represented within both gain regimes (49%/51% during On, 50%/50% during Off), (ii) the
     conscious/suppressed partition agreed with a partition by total firing rate (separating bins into
     higher/lower than median firing rate) no better than chance (Cohen's κ ≈ 0.0), and (iii) the
     conscious/suppressed states recovered by the four-state model agreed closely with those from
     the original two-state model (Fig. 2a; 80% agreement), leaving the state-resolved reconstructions
     and single-cell tuning asymmetry largely unchanged (Extended Data Figure 9). The On-Off
     fluctuations therefore did not substantially affect the inference of conscious/suppressed states.

     Alternations of conscious and suppressed states are weakly coordinated across face
     patches

     At the perceptual level, rivalry is unified: only one image is consciously seen at a time, and the
     corresponding representation is thought to be coherent across the brain. It is not clear, however,
     that this unity extends to the fast micro-epochs uncovered here. The alternation between
     conscious and suppressed states could be a coordinated, network-wide event, with regions
     switching together, or each region could alternate on its own clock, arriving at the same percept
     through independent selection. To distinguish these possibilities, we inferred the state sequence
     separately from the simultaneously recorded ML and AM populations, each with its own neurons
     and its own templates (Fig. 2a), and then predicted face features using neurons in the other patch
     (Fig. 5e). Explained variance of features was relatively low compared to Fig. 3, and for AM→ML,
     explained variance for the conscious face in state 2 was higher than that for the suppressed face;
     combining across conditions, we did find a significant separation overall between conscious
     representations in state 1 and suppressed representations in state 2 (ML→AM: 𝑝 = 3 × 10−4;
     AM→ML: 𝑝 = 9 × 10−6, t-test, see Methods). However, our results do not provide strong evidence
     for coordination across patches in generating conscious and suppressed micro-epochs.

     Comparison of two-state model to alternative models

     Several alternative mechanisms could in principle reproduce one or another signature of our
     analyses so far without reflecting a genuine, population-level temporal multiplexing of conscious
     and suppressed representations. We therefore considered a broad set of alternative models and
     subjected each, together with the recorded data, to a battery of complementary control analyses
     (Extended Data Fig. 10a, model × test matrix). Only a two-state model, in which the same
     neurons coherently alternate, within single trials, between faithfully representing the conscious
     and the suppressed face, reproduced every signature observed in the real data; each alternative
     failed at least three of the tests.

     Alternative models. We simulated four generative models of single-trial neural activity, each built
     from the same priming-derived, per-neuron response templates for the two faces of a trial (𝑟𝐴
     conscious, 𝑟𝐵 suppressed) and matched to the recorded trial structure of the real data (see
     Methods): (i) a two-state model, in which the entire population coherently alternates between the
     two faces at the ~60 ms timescale estimated from the data; (ii) a conscious-only model in which
     only the conscious face is represented throughout the trial; (iii) an averaging model, in which every
     neuron fires at a fixed rate equal to the average of its two priming templates, with no alternation;
     and (iv) an uncoordinated model, in which every neuron alternates between the conscious and
     suppressed face with the same ~60 ms dwell time as the two-state model but on its own,
     independent of the rest of the population. In every model, spike counts were generated as Poisson
     draws from the model's per-neuron, per-time-bin firing rate.

     A battery of tests. We evaluated every model, and the real data, on four tests (matrix rows in
     Extended Data Fig. 10a): (i) reconstruction of the conscious and suppressed faces from state-
     resolved activity using circular analysis (as in Fig. 2h,i); (ii) coordinated reconstruction, the
     same reconstruction after the state is inferred from one half of the population and the faces are

     The conscious-only model cannot reconstruct the suppressed face for obvious reasons, and
     therefore is clearly incompatible with our experimental findings; we therefore focus on the three
     models that represent both faces. The two-state, averaging, and uncoordinated models could all
     reconstruct the conscious face in inferred state 1 and the suppressed face in inferred state 2
     above chance using circular analysis (Extended Data Fig. 10b). This may at first seem surprising:
     how could a model with no genuine alternation, e.g., an averaging model that never represents
     either face on its own, be partitioned into two sets of time points from which noise alone
     reconstructs each pure face? Yet this is actually expected on deeper examination. In the

     between locations in neural state space, producing intermediate activity patterns. Forcing these
     transition periods into one of two states may reduce state-specific reconstruction accuracy and
     hence explained variance.

     Evidence for similar interleaved representations in prefrontal cortex

     The results so far show that inferotemporal cortex interleaves representations of the conscious
     and suppressed face. We next asked whether prefrontal cortex carries a pure representation of
     the conscious face, or an interleaved representation similar to inferotemporal cortex. We repeated
     the flash-suppression experiments in prefrontal cortex, recording from the lateral orbitofrontal
     sulcus and the infraprincipal dimple (Fig. 1e, right).

     Reconstructions were of lower quality than in inferotemporal cortex (Fig. 6a): the best possible
     explained variance, reconstructing faces from priming responses, was below 80%, compared to
     over 90% for inferotemporal cortex, and particularly low (less than 30%) for shape features (Fig.
     6b). This may reflect a lower proportion of face cells or a different feature code in prefrontal cortex.
     Despite this, the same pattern of state-dependent representation of conscious and suppressed
     stimuli held in prefrontal cortex: reconstructions during state 1 more closely resembled the
     conscious face, and those during state 2 the suppressed face, confirmed by both explained
     variance and decoding using the same data samples as for state inference (Fig. 6b-c, 𝑝 = 10−14 ,
     t-test, see Methods). Importantly, as in inferotemporal cortex, the effect held under a cross-neuron
     validation: when the states were inferred from one half of the prefrontal population and the faces
     reconstructed from the held-out half, the state-dependent separation persisted (Fig. 6d-e, 𝑝 =
     3 × 10−4, t-test, see Methods), indicating a genuine population-level alternation.

     Modulation by conscious percept was again much weaker compared to unambiguous alternation
     for average activity (Fig. 6f, 𝑝 = 4 × 10−10 , paired t-test; slope = 0.32), suggesting that, just like
     inferotemporal cortex, prefrontal cortex also does not have a pure representation of conscious
     perception for average responses. During state 1, however, modulation resembled the conscious
     stimulus (slope = 0.91) and during state 2 modulation resembled the suppressed stimulus (slope
     = -0.52). Temporal multiplexing of conscious and suppressed representations may therefore be
     a broad cortical phenomenon rather than a feature unique to inferotemporal cortex.

     Discussion

     The findings presented above reveal a temporally multiplexed code for conscious and suppressed
     visual representations. State-resolved reconstructions show that inferotemporal cortex alternates
     between complementary representational epochs: one in which the population represents the
     consciously perceived face, and another in which it represents the suppressed face. These
     representational switches in neural activity happen over an order of magnitude faster (15 Hz) than
     perceptual switches (~0.5 Hz)6,17. Single-cell analyses show that this alternation is not produced
     by separate populations of conscious- and suppressed-selective neurons, but by the same
     neurons changing which stimulus they represent across states. Importantly, during the conscious
     state, single-cell tuning to the conscious stimulus has stronger gain than tuning to the suppressed
     stimulus in the suppressed state, and a linear decoder can discriminate state 1 and state 2 across
     stimulus pairs. These states are weakly coordinated across simultaneously recorded face
     patches. Finally, prefrontal cortex shows a qualitatively similar interleaved structure. Together,
     these findings show that time provides a critical dimension for separating conscious and
     suppressed representations within cortical population activity.

     Our findings expose a limitation of searching for the neural correlates of consciousness within the
     average activity of specific brain regions, whether toward the front or back of the brain. Rather
     than finding a pure representation of conscious perception in high-level areas, we found that
     average rivalry responses remained less modulated than responses to unambiguous stimulus
     alternation, even in prefrontal cortex; this is consistent with previous studies7. The apparent weak
     modulation can now be understood as the consequence of averaging across temporally
     interleaved conscious and suppressed representations. The discovery of this temporal structure
     highlights the importance of large-scale simultaneous recordings for resolving coordinated
     population dynamics within single trials rather than relying on responses averaged across trials.

     Previous studies noted that responses during rivalry did not fully collapse to the conscious
     stimulus response, leading the authors to suspect that suppressed stimuli might still leave residual
     information in neural activity7. This possibility was later supported by the finding that suppressed
     stimuli could be decoded from inferotemporal responses2. The present work extends these
     findings by showing that neural activity not only allows discriminating between two different
     suppressed stimuli but allows reconstruction of the suppressed stimulus with high fidelity. Thus,
     the suppressed stimulus is not represented as a weak residual trace but occupies the same high-
     dimensional face space as the conscious stimulus during complementary temporal epochs, and
     both epochs rely on a shared mapping between face features and single-cell responses. The
     presence of a suppressed stimulus representation even at high levels of the cortical hierarchy
     may help explain how unconscious stimuli are able to influence behaviour18, attention19, and
     affective responses20.

     The temporal multiplexing reported here may reflect a broader coding principle by which neural
     populations organize information over time. In attention, behavioural sensitivity and frontoparietal
     activity fluctuate rhythmically, suggesting that visual information is sampled in alternating temporal
     windows rather than through a continuously stable spotlight21-23. In working memory, stimulus
     information can be expressed in brief beta- and gamma-band events, and recent work suggests
 bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.10.15.759299; this version posted October 16, 2026. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
                                                available under a CC-BY 4.0 International license.




     that frontal theta phase can modulate which remembered location is most accessible for readout,
     indicating that temporal structure can regulate when stored representations are expressed or
     accessed24-28. In value-based decision-making, neural populations in orbitofrontal cortex alternate
     on a single-trial basis between states representing the values of different choice options29. In
     spatial navigation, abrupt changes in context can produce theta-paced flickering between distinct
     hippocampal place-cell maps30. Together with our findings, these studies suggest that temporal
     structure may be a general organizing principle for encoding multiple representations within
     overlapping neural populations.

     The presence of rich representations of the suppressed stimulus raises a question for future work:
     why do representations in some micro-epochs reach awareness, whereas others do not? The fact
     that conscious and suppressed states can be discriminated by a linear decoder suggests that
     neural activity contains information not only about stimulus content, but also about whether the
     content represented in a given temporal epoch is destined for awareness or remains unconscious.
     One possibility is that this signature determines whether a sensory representation is dynamically
     routed to downstream systems involved in report, introspection, and behavioural control. Such
     routing could be controlled by recurrent interactions within visual cortex or by feedback from
     higher-order regions such as parietal cortex, the pulvinar, or other networks capable of
     coordinating perceptual states. Speculatively, suppressed epochs may allow the brain to explore
     alternative interpretations of the visual input, without being broadcast or influencing behaviour,
     whereas conscious epochs may express a working hypothesis that, through dynamic routing,
     becomes accessible to circuits that use it for introspection or actions such as report. Identifying
     the mechanisms that orchestrate these transitions, and determining why only some temporal
     segments become available to awareness, will be essential for understanding how temporal
     population dynamics give rise to conscious perception.

     Methods

     All animal procedures in this study complied with local and National Institutes of Health guidelines
     including the US National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals
     and were performed with the approval of the California Institute of Technology Institutional Animal
     Care and Use Committee (IACUC) and the University of California Berkeley Animal Care and Use
     Committee (ACUC). Human participant experiments were performed with approval of the
     California Institute of Technology Institutional Review Board (IRB).

     Targeting. Two male rhesus macaques were implanted with head posts and acrylic implants. We
     targeted face patches ML and AM in IT cortex, as well as ventrolateral prefrontal and orbitofrontal
     cortex for electrophysiological recordings. ML and AM were identified using fMRI. Monkeys were
     scanned in a 3T scanner (Siemens), as described previously31. During functional scans, MION
     contrast agent was injected to increase signal-to-noise ratio and monkeys passively viewed
     blocks of faces and blocks of other objects to identify face-selective patches in the brain, using a
     custom eight-channel coil (MGH) at isotropic 1 mm resolution. Recording chambers were
     implanted over ML and AM, and custom grids and lattices were 3D printed (3D Systems) and
     inserted into the chamber to target the desired areas. Both chamber placement and grid design
     were planned with the software Planner32. We inserted tungsten electrodes and then performed
     anatomical MRI scans to confirm correct targeting of the desired location.

     Electrophysiological recording. Single tungsten electrodes were used for scout recordings in
     the targeted areas prior to Neuropixels recordings. An Omniplex DHP system (Plexon) and
     custom analysis software written in MATLAB (MathWorks) were used for these initial recordings.
     Tungsten electrodes with an impedance of 100 kΩ (FHC) were inserted through plastic guide
     tubes (WPI), that extended 2 mm past the dura. Guide tubes were inserted through a custom 3D
     printed grid with holes designed to be in the direction of the target. After tungsten electrode
     recordings confirmed the presence of cells at the desired target, custom 3D-printed lattices were
     designed for Neuropixels recordings using the same trajectory. Subsequently, Neuropixels 1.0
     NHP probes (45 mm long probes with 4416 contacts along the shaft, of which 384 are selectable
     at any time) were inserted using a custom-machined insertion mechanism, as part of which either
     a MDO-974A microdrive (Narishige) or an ultrasonic piezo motor drive (XLA-1 series, Xeryon)
 bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.10.15.759299; this version posted October 16, 2026. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
                                                available under a CC-BY 4.0 International license.




     slowly advanced the Neuropixels probe through a stainless steel cannula (A-M systems) along a
     linear ball bearing rolling guide rail (IKO). Our insertion mechanism is described in more detail in
     13
       . All Neuropixels data were acquired using SpikeGLX (Bill Karsh, HHMI/Janelia Research
     Campus) and spike sorted using Kilosort333. Both single-units and multi-units were used for
     analyses.

     Stimuli and behavioural task. Monkeys were head-fixed and viewed an LCD screen (Asus ROG
     Swift PG43UQ) of 60 visual degree width and 44 degree height in a dark room. Stimuli were
     displayed on the screen with a 7 visual degree size while red/cyan anaglyph goggles were placed
     in front of the left and right eye of the monkey. Anaglyph goggles were custom made with filters
     (LEE filters) to match the red and green/blue emission spectrum of the screen, respectively, so
     that inputs to left and right eyes could be controlled independently. Emission spectra were
     measured using a PR-650 SpectraScan colorimeter (Photo Research). Gaze position was
     monitored using an infrared camera eye tracking system (Eyelink) sampled at 1000 Hz. The
     camera recorded one or both eyes through the red/cyan anaglyph filter.
     In the first phase of the experiment, each monkey passively viewed two sets of 96 and 49
     screening stimuli, in pseudorandom order (150 ms ON time, 150 ms OFF time) with a fixation spot
     of 0.2° diameter in the center of the screen. Screening stimuli consisted of 16 images of faces
     and 80 non-face images in the first screening set, with ~8 repeats per image, and 40 images of
     faces and 9 non-face images in the second screening set, with ~30 repeats per image. Monkeys
     received a juice reward for maintaining fixation for at least 3 s within a 0.5° rectangle centered on
     the fixation spot. Stimulus progression was contingent on fixation. For all experiments, only trials
     where monkeys maintained fixation for at least 90% of the trial duration were included for
     analyses.

     After the screening phase, one of two types of flash suppression stimulus sets was presented. In
     flash suppression, a priming stimulus is presented, followed immediately, without a blank period,
     by a binocular stimulus or another type of control stimulus (Fig. 1a). For the first type of stimulus
     set, ten face/object stimuli (five stimuli for left eye, and five stimuli for right eye) were selected
     from the 49 stimuli screening set that maximized the mean pairwise Euclidean distance between
     stimuli in neural space. The 25 combinations of the five left eye stimuli and five right eye stimuli
     were presented as stimulus pairs. For each stimulus pair, one of the two stimuli was presented
     monocularly as a priming stimulus for two seconds. The priming stimulus was followed by one of
     the two types of test stimuli, presented for four seconds: (1) a binocular stimulus, consisting of
     the priming stimulus and the other stimulus of the stimulus pair presented to the two eyes,
     respectively, referred to as the ‘binocular’ context, (2) a monocular stimulus, consisting of only
     the other stimulus of the pair that was not presented during priming, referred to as the
     ‘unambiguous’ context, used for the modulation on the x-axis of Fig. 1h and Fig. 4g. For each
     stimulus pair, both contexts were presented, and either stimulus was presented as priming
     stimulus in different trials. The new stimulus that was flashed during the post-priming test stimulus
     is referred to as the dominant stimulus, whereas the stimulus that was already present during
     priming is referred to as the suppressed stimulus. The test stimulus was followed by a 500 ms
     blank period containing only the fixation spot on a grey screen. Four stimulus pairs were presented
     with ~30 trials per condition, and the rest of the 25 stimulus pairs were presented with ~5 trials

(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made

     per condition (for image pair × which image was dominant × unambiguous / binocular = 100
     conditions). This experiment was performed in seven sessions in monkey A and three sessions
     in monkey M and analysed for Fig. 1h, Fig. 2c,d,k,l, Fig. 3a,h,i, Fig. 4g, Fig. 5c,d and Fig. 6f.

     For the second type of flash suppression stimulus set, 1500 unique faces, randomly sampled from
     face feature space, were presented. The same 1500 faces were presented in all experiments
     using this second type of stimulus. To maximize the number of unique stimuli, we did not present
     all combinations; instead, this set consisted of 750 stimulus pairs, with each of the 1500 faces
     being part of only one stimulus pair. Moreover, the priming stimulus duration and post-priming
     test stimulus duration were both reduced to one second, and the blank period after the test
     stimulus was reduced to 250 ms. As in the first experiment, both stimuli of the stimulus pair were
     presented as priming stimulus in different trials. This type of experiment did not include an
     unambiguous alternation condition. A subset of 60 faces was presented for a higher number of
     repeats (~20 trials per condition for high repeat stimulus pairs, ~2 trials per condition for the rest
     of the 750 stimulus pairs). Reconstructions were generated for this subset of 60 faces, while the
     linear regression was trained on all 1500 faces. This experiment was performed in a separate set
     of five sessions in monkey A and four sessions in monkey M, and analysed for Fig. 1g, Fig. 2b-
     j, Fig. 3a-g, Fig. 5 and Fig. 6a-e.

     Data analysis. For fitting LLR and HMM, Python version 3.11 was used. All other analyses were
     performed using MATLAB 2023b and 2025b (MathWorks). For Extended Data Fig. 5 and
     Extended Data Fig. 10 Claude Code was used in order to reduce computational runtime, and all
     generated code was reviewed for correctness by J.K.H.

     Human reports. Because button reports lag stimulus presentation, we first estimated report
     latency from the priming period, independently of the flash: for each observer, the time at which
     the probability of reporting the primed colour reached half its rise from the pre-onset baseline to
     its maximum (0.86 ± 0.05 s, mean latency ± s.e.m., Fig. 1b). The analysis window for determining
     significance of suppression was 300–1000 ms after flash onset (the same window as used for
     neural analysis, see below), shifted by this latency. Flash suppression was quantified per observer
     as a suppression index: the mean difference in report probability between the flashed and the
     priming stimulus within this window. We performed a two-sided t-test of this suppression index
     against the null hypothesis of it being 0 across all 14 human participants. The flashed percept
     was reported on 82% of binocular rivalry trials, only 5 percentage points below the 87% correct
     reporting of the unambiguous priming stimulus. The 13-percentage-point deviation from ceiling
     during priming provides an estimate of baseline report errors human participants made even when
     the percept was unambiguous. The small additional reduction during rivalry may be partially due
     to a refractory period in reporting rather than failures of perceptual suppression: rivalry reports
     followed shortly after the preceding priming report, whereas the priming report was made after a
     substantially longer response-free interval. Closely spaced responses are known to interfere with
     one another, producing a psychological refractory period or dual-task cost that reduces accuracy
     of the second response34.

     Log-likelihood ratio (LLR). For each binocular trial we computed a continuous single-trial index of
     the momentary state as the population log-likelihood ratio (LLR) between the two states of a
     Poisson mixture model with equal mixture weights (Fig. 2a). In consecutive 10-ms bins, the spike
     counts of the visually responsive IT population were evaluated under the two emission templates,
     which were computed from the mean firing rates of each neuron during the corresponding single-
     face priming trials. The LLR was computed as the difference of the population Poisson log-
     likelihoods, ℓ(𝑡) = ∑𝑖 𝑛𝑖 (𝑡) · 𝑙𝑜𝑔(𝜆𝑐𝑖 /𝜆𝑖𝑠 ) − (𝜆𝑐𝑖 − 𝜆𝑖𝑠 ) · 𝛥𝑡, where 𝑛𝑖 (𝑡) is the spike count of
     neuron 𝑖, 𝜆𝑐𝑖 and 𝜆𝑖𝑠 its template firing rates derived from priming activity when the
     conscious/suppressed face was presented unambiguously, and 𝛥𝑡 = 10 𝑚𝑠. ℓ(𝑡) is positive
     when the population evidence favours the conscious state and negative when it favours the
     suppressed state, and states were assigned based on this sign.
     To determine whether rivalry responses were better described by two states than by a single
     static response, we compared the marginal likelihood under the two-state Poisson mixture model
     (used for LLR decoding) with that under the best-fitting one-state model. The best-fitting one-state
     model was obtained by maximum-likelihood estimation, yielding a static firing-rate vector equal to
     the average response during binocular rivalry trials (Fig. 3a). For the two-state model, the two
     states were assigned equal prior probability and the marginal likelihood of the spike-count vector
     in each time bin was computed by averaging its Poisson likelihood under each of the two priming
     templates, thereby marginalizing over state assignments. The likelihood of a trial was the product
     of these marginal likelihoods across time bins. To compute the likelihood of the static model, we
     split the rivalry trials into halves, computed the average rivalry response from one half, and then
     calculated the Poisson likelihood of the spikes in the other half using this estimate as the
     underlying firing-rate vector. We then repeated this procedure with the two halves reversed. The
     two-state templates were estimated from independent priming trials and therefore did not require
     a trial split.

     Autocorrelation and LLR timescale. For each session, we computed the autocorrelation of the
     LLR at 1 ms lag resolution up to 500 ms, pooling over binocular trials. We then averaged this
     curve across 19 sessions (Fig. 2d). As a null, we also recomputed the autocorrelation of the LLR
     after permuting time bins of neural data within trials, independently for each neuron, which
     abolished all temporal structure. Both real and shuffled autocorrelation curves were normalized
     by their zero-lag value. The real autocorrelation has two regimes: below 10 ms it reflects the
     triangular autocorrelation of the 10-ms analysis window (ℓ is constant within a bin) and carries no
     dynamical information, whereas beyond 10 ms it decays with the persistence of the LLR signal.
     The shuffled autocorrelation goes to zero after the 10 ms window, as expected. Modelling the
     state as a symmetric two-state Markov chain, whose autocorrelation is 𝜌 · 𝑒 −2𝜏/𝐷 with mean dwell
     time 𝐷, we fit this form to the session-averaged autocorrelation over lags 10–90 ms (from the bin
     edge to the first zero-crossing) by least squares, with the amplitude ρ free. This mean dwell time
     of 𝐷 = 67 𝑚𝑠 corresponds to a median dwell of 𝐷 · 𝑙𝑛2 ≈ 46 𝑚𝑠 for exponentially distributed
     dwell times, approximately matching the HMM-inferred time scale described below.

     Euclidean decoding. Euclidean-distance-based decoding was performed on the predicted face
     features (Fig. 2j). We picked the 50 principal z-scored shape and appearance features that were
     predicted for a given face, and then compared the Euclidean distance of the selected features to
     the ground truth features of the selected face and the ground truth features of a given number of
     randomly chosen distractor faces. The selected face was labelled as decoded correctly if the
     distance of the predicted features was closer to its ground truth features than features of all the
     other faces. This was repeated for all 60 predicted faces to yield a decoding accuracy.
     To test for statistical significance of the Euclidean-distance-based decoding we computed for
     each high-repeat face identity the 10-way Euclidean nearest-neighbour decoding accuracy of
     the state-resolved reconstruction against the true face-feature vectors (averaged over random
     10-identity subsets), separately for state-1 (dominant/conscious) and state-2 (suppressed)
     responses and for the dominant and the suppressed face. Per identity we formed the interaction
     contrast, (dominantstate 1 – suppressedstate 1) – (dominantstate 2 − suppressedstate 2), averaged the
     two members of each rivalry pair into a single value (n = 30 pairs, the unit of inference), and
     tested it against zero with a two-sided sign-flip permutation test (10⁵ permutations, each
     randomly flipping the sign of whole pairs).

     Single-trial decoding. A cross-validated multi-class classifier was trained to read out face identity
     from the population response and applied to individual binocular trials. The classifier (a linear
     support-vector machine, 8-fold stratified cross-validation) was trained on the priming trials, using
     as features each neuron's mean firing rate over the priming response window; class labels were
     the identities of the 10 presented faces.
     The priming-trained classifier was then applied, without retraining, to binocular trials, in sliding
     200 ms windows stepped by 10 ms across the trial (Fig. 2k) or over the full stimulus window (Fig.
     2l). For every trial and window, the population response was averaged over the window and
     classified into a single predicted face. We computed conscious decoding accuracy, the fraction
     of trials on which the predicted face matched the consciously perceived face, and suppressed
     decoding accuracy, the fraction matching the suppressed face.
     We repeated the readout on state-restricted responses: within each window the population
     response was averaged only over time points assigned to a given state (state 1 or state 2), and
     trials with no time points in that state in the window were dropped.

     Decoding state 1 vs state 2. To test whether IT population activity allows distinguishing the two
     inferred states, generalizable beyond stimulus-specific tuning, we trained a linear decoder to
     discriminate the ordering of paired state 1 and state 2 responses (Fig. 4f). For each stimulus we
     took the population response vectors to that stimulus being conscious during state 1 and to that
     stimulus being suppressed during state 2. We then constructed a balanced two-class decoding
     problem: for every stimulus, we created two types of population vector concatenations: the state-
     1 response, followed by the state-2 responses, was labelled class 1, and the reversed
     concatenation, state 2, followed by state 1, was labelled class 2. Because each stimulus
     contributes identical responses to both classes and differs only in concatenation order, stimulus
     identity carries no information about the label; the only cue is whether the state-1 response is the
     first half or second half of the vector. The decoder can therefore only discriminate labels correctly
     if the state-1 and state-2 responses to the same stimulus are systematically distinguishable,
     beyond stimulus identity. We trained a linear SVM on a random half of the stimulus pairs and
     tested on the held-out half with both members of a pair kept in the same set, so that no stimulus
     appeared in both training and test. Accuracy was computed as a function of number of neurons
     by subsampling neurons, over 100 random split-and-subsample iterations per size.
