                                        1   Introduction
                                        Language model agents (Guo et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024a) increasingly move beyond isolated tasks to
                                        operate over long interaction streams (Ai et al., 2026; Asawa et al., 2026; He et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2026).
                                        In these settings, agents must adapt to distribution shifts while retaining useful experience from earlier
                                        instances in those streams. Recent agent harnesses (Guo et al., 2026a; Ning et al., 2026) enable such continual
                                        learning (Wang et al., 2024b; Shi et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2025) by maintaining persistent runtime scaffolding
                                        around a fixed large language model (LLM), often through memory (Chhikara et al., 2025; Zhang et al.,
                                        2025b; Dorovatas et al., 2026), reflection (Lingam et al., 2025; Yao et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026c), and
                                        prompts (Agrawal et al., 2026). This allows agents to improve online without updating model parameters.
                                        However, existing work typically evolves the harness state only through forward information flow. After each
                                        instance, these systems extract useful information (Chhikara et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2026; Zhang et al.,
                                        2026a) or reflect on the resulting trajectory (Lingam et al., 2025; Yao et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026c),
                                        integrate the experience into a stateful harness, and later retrieve from it (Wu et al., 2026b; Liu et al., 2026a;
                                        Zhang et al., 2026d) to guide future decisions. Nevertheless, once written, memories and reflections are rarely
                                        updated based on their effectiveness on later instances. While some methods (Chhikara et al., 2025; Suzgun
                                        et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026c) curate the harness state through operations such as add and delete, these
                                        operations primarily maintain a coherent memory or reflection bank rather than incorporate feedback from
                                        downstream use. Other methods (Cai et al., 2026; Kang et al., 2026; Lee et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026a)
                                        instead optimize the harness state using additional training or validation sets, which are unavailable under a
                                        strictly online streaming protocol. Under continual distribution shifts, outdated memories or overconfident
                                        reflections can therefore persist in the harness state and repeatedly misguide future decisions.
                                        To address this gap, we propose ReHarness to close the recurrent loop for agentic continual learning. ReHarness
                                        complements the forward information flow in existing work with a backward revision mechanism. The revision
                                        asks what information from past experience could have been captured differently to better inform the current


                                                                                                1
instance. Specifically, after each instance, we use a revision agent to revisit the reflections retrieved for that
instance. We use the resulting trajectory as feedback for revision, while requiring any new content to remain
grounded in the reflections’ source trajectories. In this way, ReHarness propagates feedback backward through
the textual harness state and carries the revised state forward to future instances. This enables online harness
adaptation under a strictly streaming protocol without instance replay or gradient-based parameter updates.
Together, forward read and write, coupled with backward revision, form a recurrent harness that can promptly
correct outdated memories or overconfident reflections under distribution shifts.
We demonstrate that ReHarness consistently improves continual learning across three benchmarks (Ai et al.,
2026; Asawa et al., 2026; He et al., 2026) and three backbone LLMs (Anthropic, 2026; Google DeepMind, 2026;
OpenAI, 2026a). In particular, ReHarness outperforms strong raw-context baselines, structured memory and
reflection methods (Chhikara et al., 2025; Lingam et al., 2025; Ouyang et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026c), and
prompt optimization approaches (Agrawal et al., 2026). These improvements are statistically significant against
all baselines, and the gains remain broadly consistent across the 16 evaluated tasks, including challenging
settings where most baselines degrade relative to their stateless backbones. Further analysis confirms that
ReHarness produces source-grounded, meaningful revisions and improves both stability and plasticity during
continual learning. Qualitative examples and harness state dynamics illustrate how ReHarness corrects early
overgeneralizations and revises outdated experience after distribution shifts. Finally, ablation studies show
that ReHarness does not require a large revision set to outperform the strongest full-history baseline, while
using roughly one-third as many tokens.
We summarize our contributions as follows:
• We propose ReHarness, which combines forward information flow with backward revision to form a recurrent
  harness for agentic continual learning in an online streaming setting.
• We show that ReHarness outperforms strong baselines across three continual learning benchmarks and
  three LLM backbones, with statistically significant gains consistent across challenging tasks.
• We demonstrate that ReHarness produces source-grounded and meaningful revisions, adapts overgeneralized
  or outdated reflections under distribution shifts, and improves stability and plasticity.


2    Related Work
Continual Learning. Continual learning (Wang et al., 2024b; Shi et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2025) aims to
adapt models to evolving data streams under distribution shifts while preventing catastrophic forgetting. For
LLMs, recent work has extended this setting to test-time adaptation (Akyürek et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2025;
Zuo et al., 2025; Feng et al., 2026) and self-evolving systems (Tao et al., 2024; Gao et al., 2026) that improve
from interaction feedback. In agentic settings, learning signals are often accumulated through memory (Zhang
et al., 2025b; Dorovatas et al., 2026; Luo et al., 2026), prompts (Agrawal et al., 2026), or persistent runtime
states (Park et al., 2023; Karten et al., 2026), enabling online adaptation while keeping the underlying LLM
parameters fixed. Recent benchmarks (Shridhar et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2024; Ai et al., 2026; Asawa et al.,
2026; Harrington et al., 2026; He et al., 2026) evaluate such systems over long interaction streams, measuring
both plasticity to distribution shifts and stability of acquired capabilities. In this work, we study agentic
continual learning under a strictly streaming protocol and improve online adaptation in memory space by
revising accumulated experience based on its downstream effectiveness.

Agent Harness. Agent harnesses (Guo et al., 2026a; Ning et al., 2026) provide runtime scaffolding around a
fixed LLM, turning text generation into goal-directed, multi-step behavior. A major line of work on agent
harnesses for continual learning focuses on memory, including how raw experiences are stored (Packer et al.,
2023; Chhikara et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026; Rajesh et al., 2026), retrieved (Liu et al., 2026b; Guo et al.,
2026b; Wu et al., 2026b; Liu et al., 2026a; Zhang et al., 2026d), and optimized (Kang et al., 2026; Karten
et al., 2026; Lee et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026c; Ma et al., 2026; Pan et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026;
Zhang et al., 2026a,b) to support future instances. Some methods optimize memory offline (Cai et al., 2026;
Ye et al., 2026), while others perform online in-context learning (Zheng et al., 2024; Sarukkai et al., 2025)
by accumulating past demonstrations. More recent approaches instead abstract experience into high-level
reflections (Lingam et al., 2025; Suzgun et al., 2026; Ouyang et al., 2026; Yao et al., 2026) and manage

3      Framework
We first motivate recurrent agent harnesses by identifying limitations of forward-only harness evolution in
§3.1, then introduce textual backward feedback through revision to address the challenges of agentic continual
learning in §3.2, and finally propose the ReHarness framework in §3.3.

3.1      From Stateful to Recurrent Agent Harnesses
An agent harness for continual learning (Ai et al., 2026; Asawa et al., 2026; Dorovatas et al., 2026; He et al.,
2026) provides stateful runtime scaffolding around a decision agent πD over a stream of instances. After
observing the previous instance trajectory τt−1 , a memory agent πM produces a reflection Rt−1 and writes it
into the current harness state ht . When the next instance arrives, the harness retrieves relevant reflections
from ht to assist πD , which responds to query xt with yt and receives reward rt from the environment. We
denote the resulting trajectory of the current instance as τt = (xt , yt , rt ). This process repeats throughout
the stream {τt }Tt=1 . Existing work (Zhao et al., 2024; Lingam et al., 2025; Ouyang et al., 2026; Zhang et al.,
2026c) thus evolves the harness state through alternating forward write (τt−1 → Rt−1 → ht ) and forward read
(ht → Rt−1 → τt ).
Figure 1 (a) illustrates this forward information flow in stateful harnesses with a simple sorting example.
After correctly sorting {3, 7, 4} in descending order at τt−1 , πM writes the reflection “put larger numbers
first” into ht . At τt , however, the query asks for ascending order. Retrieving Rt−1 therefore misleads πD ,
which produces an incorrect answer. Although πM then writes a new reflection Rt recommending “put smaller
numbers first,” the misleading Rt−1 remains in ht+1 and is retrieved again for τt+1 because the queries contain
overlapping numbers. Consequently, the same overgeneralized reflection misguides πD again.

This example shows that a stateful harness with only the forward path is incomplete for continual learning.
This forward-only reflection process does not account for the effectiveness of reflections when reused in future
instances under distribution shifts. Misleading reflections can persist in the harness state and propagate errors
through later retrievals. We instead propose a recurrent agent harness that combines forward information
flow with backward feedback from future instances.

3.2   Textual Backward Feedback through Revision
Agentic continual learning under a strictly streaming setting poses unique challenges for backward feedback.
First, each instance is observed only once, so we cannot return to past instances to collect additional
counterfactual outcomes for offline optimization (Cai et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026; Pan et al., 2026). Second,
we do not have additional validation sets to select among candidate harness updates (Kang et al., 2026; Lee
et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026b). Third, frontier API-based agents (Anthropic, 2026; Google DeepMind, 2026;
OpenAI, 2026a) do not expose model parameters, precluding gradient-based updates. On the other hand,
prior work (Pryzant et al., 2023; Shinn et al., 2023; Yuksekgonul et al., 2024) shows that natural language
feedback can provide an effective learning signal without parameter updates. We therefore realize backward
feedback in the textual harness state, allowing the harness to adapt online as the interaction stream evolves.
Specifically, we introduce a revision agent πR that revisits reflections used for the current instance based
on their observed effectiveness. Using the same sorting example in Figure 1 (b), after observing the current

3.3   ReHarness: Closing the Recurrent Loop
We now instantiate this recurrent harness framework as ReHarness in Figure 1 (b).

Step 1: Forward Read. Let ht = {Ri }i<t denote the current harness state. Given query xt , we provide the
decision agent πD with the top-k relevant reflections retrieved from ht :
                      It = {i1 , . . . , ik } = Retrieval(xt , ht , k),       yt = πD (xt , {Ri }i∈It ) .        (1)
The environment then provides reward rt , forming the instance trajectory τt = (xt , yt , rt ).

Step 2: Backward Revision. After observing τt , the revision agent πR independently revisits each retrieved
reflection using its source trajectory τi and its effectiveness on the current instance τt :
                                           Ri′ = πR (Ri , τi ; τt ) ,       ∀i ∈ It .                            (2)
Importantly, τt serves only as feedback for assessing and revising Ri . Any new content in Ri′ must remain
grounded in its source trajectory τi to prevent information leakage. The revision may therefore incorporate
evidence missed in τi , correct unsupported hypotheses in Ri , or refine the conditions under which the reflection
should apply. Replacing the retrieved reflections with their revised versions while retaining all unretrieved
reflections yields the intermediate state h′t .

Step 3: Forward Write.     Finally, the memory agent πM reflects on τt to produce a new reflection:
                                    Rt = πM (τt ; {Ri′ }i∈It ) ,          ht+1 = h′t ⊕ Rt .                      (3)
We prompt πM to explore operational evidence and retain only well-supported interpretations or strategies.
The revised reflections {Ri′ }i∈It serve as demonstrations for πM , while the prompt encourages Rt to capture
complementary perspectives not covered by those demonstrations.

4     Experiment

4.1    Experiment Setup
Baselines.     We compare ReHarness against the following strong baselines.
• Mem0 (Chhikara et al., 2025) maintains atomic memories as harness state, retrieving top-k entries for the
  decision agent and incrementally extracting memories after each instance.
• RBank (Ouyang et al., 2026) maintains an append-only bank of reusable reflections as harness state and
  retrieves top-k reflections for each instance.
• DoT (Lingam et al., 2025) generates diverse reflections from the top-k retrieved prior experiences and uses
  them as the runtime harness for the current instance.
• ACE (Zhang et al., 2026c) maintains a global reflection playbook as its harness state and curates it through
  add, update, delete, and merge operations for coherence.
• GEPA-Online adapts GEPA (Agrawal et al., 2026) to our strictly online streaming setting, preserving reflective
  optimization of the decision prompt as persistent harness state, but replaces the original validation-based
  population search with sequential updates from each observed trajectory.
• Full Raw uses all prior instance trajectories in the stream as harness context, while Top-K Raw retrieves only
  the k most relevant trajectories for in-context learning.
We evaluate all methods with three frontier LLM backbones: Claude Opus 4.8 (Anthropic, 2026), GPT-
5.5 (OpenAI, 2026a), and Gemini 3.7 Flash (Google DeepMind, 2026), using the same backbone for all
agents within each method. We use each model’s default generation configuration with high thinking effort
and exclude hidden reasoning traces from all harness operations. Retrieval uses the top five entries ranked
by Qwen3-Embedding-8B (Zhang et al., 2025a) and applies the same query-to-query comparison across all
methods. We provide the prompts used by ReHarness in §A.

Datasets. We evaluate ReHarness against baselines on three continual learning benchmarks under a strictly
online streaming protocol, with no training or validation set and no repeated trials.
• CL-Bench (Asawa et al., 2026) contains six tasks spanning cohort studies, codebase adaptation (Jimenez
  et al., 2024), database exploration (Hou et al., 2026), sales prediction, blind spectrum monitoring, and
  exploitable poker. Each task contains 12 to 120 multi-round instances with explicit distribution shifts and
  task-specific evaluation metrics.
• MemoryBench (Ai et al., 2026) contains long-input, long-output generation tasks across multiple domains.
  We use its five English tasks from HelloBench (Que et al., 2024) and WritingBench (Wu et al., 2026c),
  covering academic, creative, and political writing, with 17 to 47 single-round instances per task, evaluated
  by GPT-5.6-Sol (OpenAI, 2026b).
• MemoryArena (He et al., 2026) contains five domains spanning travel planning (Xie et al., 2024), web
  shopping (Yao et al., 2022), progressive web search (Chen et al., 2026), and formal reasoning in mathematics

Across these benchmarks, we standardize each task as a continual learning stream and each constituent
interaction unit as an instance (i.e., τt ). ReHarness and all baselines maintain harness state across instances
within a stream and reset it between streams. To compare performance across tasks with different native
metrics, we follow Asawa et al. (2026) and report normalized gain, (rmethod − rstateless )/(rmax − rstateless ),
averaged over each stream. Here, rmax denotes the maximum achievable reward under each task’s native metric.
This metric quantifies the improvement over the stateless backbone agent from learning prior experience
within the stream. We repeat each experiment over three independently sampled stream rollouts. Within each
rollout, all methods share the same benchmark initialization and random seed, then evolve independently.

 4.2                ReHarness Consistently Improves Agentic Continual Learning
Overall Performance. Table 1 reports the mean normalized gain across task streams for each benchmark–
backbone combination. ReHarness achieves the highest overall normalized gain of 0.240 and ranks first or
tied in eight of the nine combinations. Consistent with Asawa et al. (2026), raw-context methods are strong
baselines: Top-K Raw and Full Raw outperform most structured memory and reflection methods on average,
particularly on CL-Bench and MemoryArena. Reflection-based methods, such as RBank, DoT, and ACE, are
less consistent, with some settings yielding negative gains. Similarly, GEPA-Online degrades substantially on
MemoryBench with Gemini, suggesting that prompt optimization alone is less robust for continual learning.
To assess statistical significance, we compare ReHarness against each baseline using two-sided paired Wilcoxon
signed-rank tests with Bonferroni correction, treating each seeded task stream as one observation. ReHarness
significantly outperforms all baselines (padj < 0.05), including the strong raw-context baselines Top-K Raw
(padj = 0.039) and Full Raw (padj = 0.043). Overall, these results suggest that revising reflections with
ReHarness is more effective than retaining raw history or relying on forward-only accumulation.

Task-Level Results. We next present the task-level breakdown for each benchmark in Figure 2, averaged
across the three backbone LLMs. The gains vary substantially across tasks, with many methods showing
marginal or even negative improvements over their stateless backbones. On CL-Bench, ReHarness leads or
remains close to the best on most tasks, but shows only marginal gains on exploitable poker. On MemoryBench,
while most methods perform well on HelloBench (HB-Academic and HB-Creative), only ReHarness achieves
consistent gains across WritingBench (WB-Academic, WB-Creative, and WB-Politics). On MemoryArena,
Mem0 is notably strong on travel planning, and GEPA-Online performs well on progressive web search, while
ReHarness remains consistently competitive across all five domains. A closer examination suggests that,
unlike the more deterministic codebase and database settings, exploitable poker is more stochastic: hand-level
rewards can be weakly coupled with strategy quality due to hidden cards and chance. Raw-context baselines
are less exposed to this credit-assignment issue because they preserve the original hand trajectories and
defer their interpretation to the decision agent at each new hand. By contrast, ReHarness is less effective
when downstream rewards provide a noisy signal for backward revision, and benefits most when feedback is
diagnostic for revising reusable reflections. The full raw results are in §A.

Token Efficiency. We further examine the token consumption and API cost of ReHarness. Despite requiring
one reflection call for forward write and k revision calls for backward revision, in addition to the decision calls
shared by all methods, ReHarness consumes fewer total tokens than the two strong raw-context baselines.
This is because compact reflections substantially reduce the repeated decision-time context cost of raw
trajectories, especially for long, multi-round instances. Across one rollout over all CL-Bench tasks with
Claude, ReHarness consumes ∼37.19M input and ∼4.38M output tokens, totaling ∼41.57M. By comparison,
Top-K Raw consumes ∼52.20M input and ∼0.46M output tokens (∼52.66M total), while Full Raw consumes
∼141.68M input and ∼0.44M output tokens (∼142.12M total), or 1.3× and 3.4× as many total tokens as
ReHarness, respectively. Thus, although ReHarness generates more output tokens, its input-token savings
more than offset this overhead in total consumption. With prompt caching and Claude API pricing, the
corresponding costs are approximately $85 for Top-K Raw, $254 for ReHarness, and $314 for Full Raw.
ReHarness therefore remains substantially cheaper than Full Raw while achieving a higher normalized gain
(0.217 vs. 0.191), whereas Top-K Raw is cheaper but achieves a lower gain (0.165).


5     Analysis

5.1   Is ReHarness Revision Source-Grounded?
We first examine whether backward revision in Equation 2 remains grounded in its source trajectory τi or
imports information from τt . Following Min et al. (2023), we decompose each revised reflection Ri′ into factual
claims using GPT-5.6-Sol (OpenAI, 2026b). We classify each claim as: (1) grounded, if entailed by τi ; (2)
leaked, if not entailed by τi but entailed by τt ; or (3) unsupported, if entailed by neither. Table 2 reports
the average rates across CL-Bench tasks for each backbone and their average change per revision. Across all
backbones, over 95% of claims remain source-grounded, with leakage at 0.8–2.1% and unsupported claims
at 0.5–2.6%. Moreover, revision changes these rates only marginally, with grounding decreasing by at most
0.3% per revision. These results indicate that the backbone LLMs largely follow the grounding constraint as
intended, and ReHarness uses τt primarily as feedback rather than as a source of leaked information.

5.2    Is ReHarness Capable of Revision?
We next validate whether ReHarness revises the harness state as intended. The goal of revision is to use
downstream feedback to improve previously learned experience for future instances. We therefore consider a
counterfactual replay that modifies the online streaming protocol. After revising the harness state into h′t
based on the completed trajectory τt , we reuse h′t to assist the decision agent on the same instance. This differs
from ReHarness, which applies h′t only to the next streamed instance τt+1 . Importantly, h′t remains grounded
in past trajectories and uses τt only as feedback on their effectiveness. The replay thus asks: given the current
outcome, what information already available in past trajectories should have been captured differently to
better inform that instance?
Table 3 reports task-level normalized gains on CL-Bench with Claude for ReHarness, counterfactual replay,
and a no-revision baseline that removes the revision agent πR and retains only forward information flow.
Counterfactual replay substantially improves over ReHarness across all tasks. This suggests that revision
can recover or correct information from past trajectories using current feedback, producing a more effective
harness state had those revisions been available beforehand. Hence, counterfactual replay confirms that
the backward mechanism produces meaningful revisions. However, it violates the online streaming protocol.
ReHarness instead applies the revised state h′t only to the next instance τt+1 , using feedback from τt to
improve future instances. Compared with the no-revision baseline, ReHarness achieves higher gains across all
tasks, especially in database exploration, sales prediction, and blind spectrum monitoring. This indicates that
backward revision remains beneficial when applied only to subsequent instances in the stream.

5.3    What Does ReHarness Revise during Continual Learning?

examples from CL-Bench with Claude in Figure 3 to examine what it revises. Figure 3 (a) shows an early-stage
example from blind spectrum monitoring, where the agent infers persistent transmitters from sequential
spectrum scans. After observing τ2 , the memory agent πM overgeneralizes the evidence and recommends

the ground-truth occupied spectrum, both missing real transmitters and hallucinating nonexistent ones hurt
performance. When this reflection is retrieved from h3 for τ3 , the decision agent πD follows the speculative
guidance, adds unobserved transmitters, and receives a lower reward. Using this outcome as backward

retaining confirmed peaks over adding unconfirmed ones,” producing h′3 . When the revised reflection is

how ReHarness corrects an early overgeneralization before it misguides future decisions.

5.4      How Does ReHarness Revise during Continual Learning?
We then investigate how ReHarness revises accumulated experience during continual learning. Following Asawa
et al. (2026), we decompose normalized gain on CL-Bench into stability and plasticity. Stability measures gain
on the first instance after a distribution shift, before post-shift feedback is available. Plasticity measures gain
on subsequent instances before the next shift, where methods can adapt to the current distribution. Because
the two categories contain different numbers of instances, their absolute gains are not directly comparable. As
shown in Table 4, Mem0, RBank, DoT, ACE, and GEPA-Online exhibit negative stability gains on average,
indicating that their forward-only harness states can become detrimental under distribution shifts. In contrast,
ReHarness achieves the highest stability gain on average and the highest plasticity gain for every backbone
LLM. These results suggest that backward revision enables more robust transfer across shifts while supporting
effective adaptation once post-shift feedback becomes available, as illustrated in Figure 3.

through backward revision. In Figure 4 (a), diagonal heatmap entries mark new reflections, while later
non-zero entries in the same row indicate revisions. We measure revision magnitude by character-level edit

 5.5                 What Makes ReHarness Effective?
 Finally, we conduct ablations to identify what makes ReHarness effective. In Figure 4 (b), we vary the
 number of retrieved and subsequently revised reflections, k, on a log scale for ReHarness and Top-K Raw on
 CL-Bench with Claude. Top-K Raw approaches Full Raw as k reaches 20, suggesting diminishing returns
 from additional in-context examples. For ReHarness, normalized gain rises sharply from k = 1 to 5, remains
 relatively stable for k ∈ {5, 10, 20}, and declines for larger k. This suggests that overly large k can introduce
 unnecessary revision and potential reflection drift while increasing computational cost. ReHarness therefore
 does not require a large revision set. We fix k = 5 throughout the main experiments and report this sweep as
 a post-hoc sensitivity analysis.
 Furthermore, we ablate two components of ReHarness. The effect of removing backward revision is already
 established in Table 3. Removing the revised reflections {Ri′ }i∈It used as demonstrations for πM in Equation 3
 reduces the normalized gain on CL-Bench with Claude from 0.217 to 0.191, indicating that revised experience
 helps guide subsequent reflection generation.


 6                  Discussion
In this paper, we propose ReHarness for agentic continual learning beyond forward-only harness evolution.
ReHarness complements forward read and write with backward revision, using downstream feedback to revise
past reflections while grounding new content in their source trajectories. Across three continual learning
benchmarks and three LLM backbones, ReHarness consistently outperforms strong baselines, with statistically
significant gains across challenging tasks. Further analyses show that ReHarness produces source-grounded
and meaningful revisions, corrects overgeneralized or outdated reflections under distribution shifts, and
improves stability and plasticity. Together, our results highlight recurrent harness adaptation as a promising
direction for continually refining accumulated experience under a strictly streaming setting. Despite these
gains, ReHarness underperforms on exploitable poker in CL-Bench, where downstream rewards provide noisy
signals for backward revision. Moreover, while ReHarness is more token-efficient than the strongest full-history
raw-context baseline, it still stores raw trajectories for future revision, incurring additional storage overhead.
We aim to address these limitations in future work.
